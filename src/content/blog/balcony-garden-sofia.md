My apartment in Sofia has two balconies, and they could hardly be more different. One faces west and gets a few hours of evening sun. The other faces south and bakes for most of the day. Same flat, first floor of an eight-floor building, two different climates.

This spring I planted both, deliberately as two separate gardens that still feel like they belong to the same home. The brief I gave myself was simple: low to medium maintenance, mostly ready-grown plants rather than seeds, and daily watering accepted on the sunny side in summer. I already had a Gardena drip irrigation kit from the year before, and I planned to plant between late April and about 10 May.

I planted in late May, and almost nothing went exactly to plan. This post covers the planning, the shopping, planting day and how the summer went, including the parts that went sideways.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Read the light before you pick a single plant. Compass bearing, floor height and the buildings around you decide more than any plant list does.
> - Railing planters are shallow by design: everything I found topped out around 18 to 23 cm. Water-retaining crystals helped, but the drip kit I already had did the real work, and both were far cheaper than hunting for deeper planters.
> - Balcony plants in Bulgaria are a spring-only product, and much of it sells out by May. Have a backup in mind for every plant, and expect to split orders across several shops.
> - Bulgarian plant names can mislead. The edible purslane and the flowering moss rose share a name, and I nearly ordered the wrong one.
> - Shade-tolerant doesn't mean it can live without sun. My shady foliage planter died over the summer.
> - Cheap soil was the one saving I'd undo. Next year every pot gets a better mix.

## Reading the light: west at 271°, south at 193°

The two numbers that shaped the whole project were the bearings. The west balcony faces 271°, almost exactly west, and its railing gets roughly 2.5 to 4.5 hours of direct sun, all of it in the afternoon and evening. The south balcony faces 193°, a touch west of due south, and gets strong sun for most of the day.

On the first floor, the bearing tells you less than you'd hope. How much of that sun actually reaches the railing depends on the buildings around you, so the number worth having is the hours of direct sun the railing gets.

In practice, that split the plant list in two. The west balcony needed plants that are happy with a few hours of sun and some shade: herbs, softer trailing flowers and foliage. The south balcony needed plants that want heat and full sun all day, and it needed them in containers that dry out fast. Most of what follows comes back to that.

## Two plans for one apartment

I wanted the balconies to feel different but coordinated: one softer and mixed, one bold and floral. The containers were already there, so the plan had to fit them:

| Container | Size | West | South |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Railing planter | 50 × 20 × 16.7 cm | 6 | 7 |
| Round railing pot | ø25 cm, 16 cm deep | 5 | 0 |
| Raised planter on legs | about 100 × 30 × 18 cm | 1 | 0 |
| Big floor planter | 60 × 30 × 24 cm | 1 (in the shade) | 1 |

I also had a short list of things I didn't want. No strawberries, ivy or geraniums on the west side, and no lush, all-green look on the south.

### The west balcony: softer and mixed

The five round pots became mostly herbs: basil, parsley (from seed), chocolate mint, rosemary and fragrant pinks (dianthus). The mint gets a pot to itself, because mint takes over any container it shares.

The six railing planters each pair a main plant with a companion:

| Planter | Main | Companion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Diascia (seed) | Bacopa |
| 2 | Nemesia (seed) | Bacopa |
| 3 | Thyme | Oregano |
| 4 | Variegated sage | Chives (seed) |
| 5 | Osteospermum ×2 | Tom Thumb strawflower (seed) |
| 6 | Gazania | Tom Thumb strawflower (seed) |

**The raised planter was meant to be the kitchen garden.** All from seed: parsley, chives and Swiss chard, plus a lettuce mix on seed tape. In the end I ran out of time and it stayed empty all summer.

**The shady floor planter was meant to be the anchor.** Two heucheras, one "Prairie Fire" sedge (Carex) and one golden euonymus (Euonymus japonicus "Aureus"). It was supposed to be the plant-and-forget part of the design and the part most likely to still be there next year. It didn't make it through the summer.

One thing to know about mixing seeds with ready plants: the seed-sown companions (diascia, nemesia, strawflower) take 6 to 8 weeks to flower from seed. Those planters look sparse at first, and the ready plants carry the display until the seeds catch up.

### The south balcony: bold and floral

The south side is simpler, because the conditions are harsher and fewer plants enjoy them:

| Planter | Plant | Per planter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, 2 | Calliope geranium (cascading) | 2 |
| 3, 4 | Trixi calibrachoa | 3 |
| 5 | Gazania | 3 |
| 6 | Calliope geranium | 2 |
| 7 | Moss rose | from seed |

**The big floor planter is edibles only.** A compact cherry tomato in the middle with a stake, two basil plants around it, then thyme and oregano at the edges.

Gazania, on both balconies, has a habit worth knowing before you panic: the flowers close in the evening and on cloudy days. Deadheading keeps it blooming.

## The railing planter depth rabbit hole

The south balcony's weak point is the railing planters. At 16.7 cm deep, a shallow box of soil in full sun all day dries out fast, so my first idea was to find deeper ones. I had an AI agent pull the dimensions for everything I could find, and this is what came back:

| Option | Depth | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| elho Vibia Campana Easy Hanger XXL 70 | 23.7 cm (69 × 26.8 cm) | Not on amazon.de, the only Amazon store usable from Bulgaria |
| Lechuza Balconera | about 12.7 cm | Self-watering, but the reservoir eats the depth, so it's shallower than what I have |
| elho noa Balcony Trough | about 1 cm more than mine | Barely deeper at all |
| GarPet with water reservoir (50, 60 or 80 cm long) | 18.5 cm | Effectively the same as mine |

Railing planters are shallow for a reason. A deep box full of wet soil hanging off a railing is heavy, it acts as a lever on the railing, and it catches the wind. That's why everything I found tops out somewhere around 18 to 23 cm. The extra few centimetres buy maybe one or two days of water, which doesn't change much on a balcony that needs watering daily in summer.

The fix was cheaper than any of the planters. I kept the ones I had and mixed water-retaining crystals (hydrogel) into the soil of every south planter, with the Gardena drip kit I'd bought the year before doing the daily watering. A 30 g pack of crystals cost about 1.33 €.

## Shopping for balcony plants in Bulgaria in May

Ready-grown balcony plants in Bulgaria are a spring product, and they sell out fast. By May, a lot of what I wanted showed as sold out, and some of it was sold out everywhere I looked.

The orders ended up spread like this:

- **SortoviSemena.bg** for the flower plants and most of the seeds.
- **AgroGradina.bg** for the herb plants, the heucheras, perlite and the crystals.
- **A separate nursery order** for the euonymus and the sedge.
- **Bauhaus** for the cherry tomato, about 0.75 €.

I checked about ten other places too, from GradinaMax.bg and Semenata.bg to HotFarm.eu for hot peppers, Pro Plants Market (a physical garden centre in Sofia) and OLX.bg, where local growers sell surplus seedlings. No single shop had everything, so splitting the order wasn't a choice.

Herb and vegetable seedlings like hot peppers and cherry tomatoes are mostly sold in-store and rarely online. If chives are the problem, supermarkets (Kaufland, Billa, Lidl) sometimes have potted chives in the fresh herb section.

### The substitution chain

This is how the plan actually changed:

| Planned | What happened | Ended up with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bacopa | Sold out everywhere, so I swapped to trailing lobelia | It came back in stock: bacopa after all |
| Rocket | Not a stock-out: rocket bolts fast once the heat arrives | Swiss chard (handles heat, cut-and-come-again, colourful stems) |
| Chives as plants | No ready plants anywhere | Chives from seed |
| Hot pepper | No plants online | Rosemary (perennial, compact, useful in the kitchen) |
| Moss rose ×4 (south planter 6) | Couldn't find any. Planned 3 more gazanias, which meant too many gazanias, so verbena instead, which then sold out too | Calliope geranium ×2 |
| Verbena ×3 (south planter 3) | Sold out | Trixi calibrachoa ×3 |

The moss rose row is my favourite. I gave up on it as plants, and it still ended up on the balcony, sown from seed in planter 7 under a different Bulgarian name. More on that in a moment.

### Swaps I said no to

Not every substitute was a good one:

- **Photinia instead of the euonymus.** Photinia grows to 3 to 5 m and needs sun for its red new growth. That's wrong for a shady 24 cm planter.
- **Allium christophii instead of chives.** It's an ornamental bulb, not something you eat. It blooms once for two or three weeks, then dies back, and its 40 to 50 cm stems would flop over.
- **Bacopa instead of verbena on the south side.** Bacopa stops flowering and wilts in intense direct sun. It belongs on the west balcony, which is where it went.
- **Seven gazanias in total.** Too repetitive.

### The purslane near-miss

At one point my cart held **Органик Тученица**, which I'd added as the flowering moss rose. It's actually organic purslane, *Portulaca oleracea*: the edible one, the kind you put in a salad.

The flower I wanted is **декоративна тученица**, "decorative" тученица, which is *Portulaca grandiflora*. The two share a Bulgarian root name but are completely different plants, and the only reliable check is the Latin name on the listing.

The seeds I sowed in south planter 7, sold as **калдъръмче кичесто**, turned out to be *Portulaca grandiflora* too. So the moss rose I couldn't find as plants ended up in the cart anyway, under a third name.

### Check every plant on delivery

When you order mixed lots from several shops, check each plant when it arrives. I took photos of the deliveries and went through them one by one, with the AI agent comparing each plant against what I'd ordered.

Most of it was right: magenta calibrachoa with a yellow throat, orange gazania (half-closed, which is normal in the evening), bushy oregano, lavender bacopa. The chocolate mint was the odd one out. It was mint, but bright green, without the darker, brown-tinged stems chocolate mint usually has. It might just be young, or it might be a nursery mix-up. Either way, delivery day is when you can still ask the shop.

## Soil without overthinking it

Soil is easy to overthink. Three requirements shaped the choice:

- The bags had to be small enough to carry up the stairs. That ruled out the 225 to 250 L bales, which weigh around 30 kg.
- I was reusing about half of the old soil, so I only needed roughly half the original volume.
- Everything would be mixed anyway: 50/50 old and new soil, plus perlite and the crystals.

These are the options I compared:

| Product | Shop | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SULIFLOR SF2, 225 L | AgroGradina | A 25 to 35 kg bale. Ruled out by weight alone |
| Bellefiori peat-based universal, 70 L | Mr. Bricolage | About 6.39 € (about 0.09 €/L). Peat-based, better for containers |
| Floraterra soil-based, 50 L | Mr. Bricolage | About 5.36 € (about 0.11 €/L). Contains bark compost, compacts more |
| Lactofol Botanika balcony flowers, 10 L | Praktiker | The best mix of the lot (peat, coco coir, perlite, zeolite), but only in small bags |
| COMPO universal, 20 L | Praktiker | Listed, not actually in stock |
| Biotsvet universal, 20 L | Praktiker | In stock and easy to carry |

The final call was a cheap, peat-based universal mix in 20 L bags, five or six of them. Three reasons:

**A premium mix adds little when you're diluting it.** Once it's 50/50 with old soil, with perlite and crystals mixed in and liquid fertiliser on top, the fancy formulation mostly disappears.

**"For flowering plants" on the bag barely matters.** The difference from a universal mix is a small dose of pre-mixed fertiliser that runs out within weeks. The liquid fertiliser is what actually drives flowering.

**Peat-based beats soil-based in shallow containers.** A peat mix stays airy and compacts less than a soil and bark blend, which matters in a 16.7 cm planter.

In hindsight, this is the decision I'd change. The reasoning sounded fine in May, but the cheap mix turned out mediocre (more on that below).

For the old soil, the rule is simple. If it crumbles and smells earthy, reuse it. If it smells sour, stays waterlogged or is full of dead roots, throw it out.

Feeding is two products only, one for flowering plants and one for tomatoes and vegetables: every two weeks from June, monthly for the shady planter.

## Planting day

The prep plan was the same for every container:

1. Clear out dead material and debris.
2. Check the drainage holes.
3. Reuse old soil only if it passes the smell test.
4. Mix old and new soil roughly 50/50.
5. Add perlite, about one part to five parts soil.
6. Add crystals to every south planter.
7. Leave about 2 cm below the rim so water has somewhere to sit.

The planting order ran roughly from the most permanent to the most exposed:

1. The shady floor planter, because perennials are plant and forget.
2. The herb pots. The raised planter was next on the list, but I never got to it.
3. The west railing planters, ready plants first with the seeds sown alongside.
4. The south edible planter: tomato in the middle with its stake, herbs around the edges.
5. The south railing planters last.

The watering plan follows the light. The west side gets watered every 3 to 5 days in spring and every 1 to 3 days in peak summer, with the shady planter needing it less often. The south railing planters need water daily in summer, and in a heatwave I check them twice a day. That's where the drip kit earns its place.

## How the summer went

Mostly to plan. Everything I actually planted survived the summer and bloomed when it was supposed to, with one exception, and one planter never got planted at all.

**Basil was the biggest winner.** It grew faster than anything else I planted, and it kept going all summer.

**The drip kit mattered more than the crystals.** The crystals did help, but the Gardena drip irrigation was what kept the south railing planters going through the heat. It's also something I want to improve next year.

**The shady floor planter died.** The heucheras, the sedge and the euonymus didn't get enough light in that corner. I'd planned for shade, but shade-tolerant plants still need some sun, and that spot had too little of it.

**The raised planter stayed empty.** I ran out of time and never planted it, so the kitchen garden (parsley, chives, Swiss chard and lettuce) waits for next year.

**The soil was mediocre.** The cheap universal mix did the job, but only just, and it's the first thing I'm upgrading. I'm considering mixing in organic compost for next season.

## What happens over the winter

I spent a fair bit of the planning on winter hardiness, and most of it turned out not to matter. Several of the herbs (mint, chives, thyme, oregano, sage) would probably survive a Sofia winter in their pots, but I'm emptying every pot this autumn to replace the soil, so nothing stays out.

The basil, which grew the most, becomes pesto. The other herbs, rosemary included, get dried and used through the winter. The geraniums are the only plants I'm trying to keep: they go down to the basement to overwinter. Everything else goes on the compost, and in spring I start again with new plants.

So in practice the balconies are a yearly project. The whole thing gets rebuilt every May, and the shady corner needs a new plan.

## Should you bother planning it this carefully?

With two balconies pointing in different directions, yes. The planning paid off before anything went into the soil: it stopped me putting bacopa in full sun, Photinia in the shade and money into deeper planters that would have bought me a day or two of water. With one balcony and one exposure, a lighter version (the bearing, the sun hours and a backup for each plant) gets you most of the way.

The lessons I'd pass on:

- **Start with the light.** Get the bearing and count the hours of direct sun before you write a plant list.
- **Accept the shallow planters, and get the drip right.** Crystals help, but drip irrigation does more than a few extra centimetres of depth ever could.
- **Shop early and have a backup for everything.** By May, the plan is whatever is still in stock.
- **Read the Latin name.** Especially in Bulgarian, where тученица can be a flower or a salad.
- **Check every plant the day it arrives.** It's the only time the shop can still fix it.
- **Shade plants still need light.** Check how much sun the darkest corner really gets before you fill it.
- **Don't economise on soil.** The cheap mix was the one saving I'd undo.

Next May it all starts again in better soil: new plants, a rethink for the shady corner and, this time, something in the raised planter. Until then, there's pesto.
